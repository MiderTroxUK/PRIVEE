\subsection{Why ALTEN Opened This Subject}
\label{sec:pourquoi}

The Smart Green Supply Chain programme had, by the start of this placement, built a
credible set of components: a routing module, a supplier risk scorer, a carbon
quantification module, a transport interface, and the DISCO twin that ties them together.
Each answers a question of the form \emph{what is the state of this part of the chain?}

None answers the question that comes next. Once the state is known, a coordinator still has
to decide where to spend a finite amount of attention this week, and that decision is made
on a signal none of those modules produces: how urgent each node is. In practice that signal
comes from people. A buyer says a supplier is critical; a plant says a line is at risk. The
digital twin records the consequence of those judgments without ever evaluating the
judgments themselves.

That is a gap with a cost attached. Behavioural research on urgency shows the signal is not
neutral. Zhu, Yang and Hsee demonstrate across five controlled experiments that people
systematically favour imminent low-value tasks over distant high-value ones, and that the
bias persists even when they are told about it~\autocite{zhu2018mere}. Field studies find the
same pattern under workload: operators complete small easy tasks first, at a measurable cost
to overall performance~\autocite{rusou2020psychology,diwa2020task}. If a digital twin
consumes a human urgency declaration as ground truth, it inherits that bias and then
propagates it across the network.

\subsection{The Cost of the Two Errors}

The two directions of error are not symmetric, which is what makes the problem worth
instrumenting rather than tolerating.

Over-declaration is expensive but visible. A node declared more urgent than it is attracts
expedited transport, premium capacity and coordinator attention, all of which are finite and
all of which were taken from somewhere else. In a shared or mutualised logistics setting the
cost lands on another actor entirely~\autocite{montreuil2011toward,li2020exploring}.

Under-declaration is cheaper in the moment and far more dangerous. A node whose real
situation is worse than anyone has said generates no alert, appears in no priority list and
consumes no attention. The failure surfaces only when it becomes a missed delivery, by which
point the window in which it could have been prevented has closed. Clinical triage, where
this asymmetry has been studied for decades, reaches the same conclusion: under-triage costs
a life where over-triage costs a queue~\autocite{kim2020admission,mackway2013emergency}.

\subsection{The Problem, Stated}
\label{sec:question}

\begin{definitionBox}
\noindent\textbf{Problem statement.}

\medskip

A supply chain digital twin can compute the physical state of every node, and it can record
the urgency a human declares for each of them. It does not compare the two. The declared
signal is known to be biased, the computed signal is available, and the difference between
them is exactly the quantity that would tell a coordinator which node is being mismanaged
rather than merely which node is struggling.

\medskip

\noindent\emph{Can the gap between declared and computed urgency be measured reliably
enough, inside a working digital twin, to be acted upon rather than merely observed?}
\end{definitionBox}

The word \emph{reliably} carries the weight. Producing a number that expresses the
difference between two other numbers is trivial. Establishing that the number means
something, that it predicts outcomes better than the raw indicators a coordinator already
has, and that it is not simply measuring its own inputs, is the actual engineering problem.
Section~\ref{sec:realisation} shows that this distinction is not academic: the system
produced the number without difficulty and then failed the reliability test, for a reason
that took a dedicated experiment to identify.
