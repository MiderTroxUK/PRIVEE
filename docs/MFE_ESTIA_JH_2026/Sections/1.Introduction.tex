A supply chain coordinator opens the week with a list of nodes and a finite amount of
attention. Somewhere on that list is the supplier that will cause next month's line
stoppage. Nothing on the screen says which one it is.

The job has changed shape and the tooling has not followed. A digital twin manages
\emph{tasks}: it records what each node has to do, how far along it is, and what state its
resources are in. What a coordinator actually has to manage is the \emph{urgency} of those
tasks, meaning the order in which a finite amount of attention is spent on them.
Figure~\ref{fig:couche} places the two layers side by side, and this report is about the
second one.

The reason that middle box stays empty is that the decision inside it is multi-criteria: cost
against deadline against supplier reliability against carbon. Supply chains are monitored
through indicators that each measure one criterion at a time, so when the tooling cannot
combine them, the coordinator combines them by judgment. Judgment is then the real decision
layer of the system, and it is the layer nobody measures yet.

The consequences are not evenly distributed. Declaring more urgency than a situation
warrants consumes shared transport and production capacity that another flow needed.
Declaring less leaves a risk that nobody is watching, and by the time it becomes visible the
window to act on it has closed. The second error is the more expensive, and it is the harder
one to detect, precisely because an under-declared risk generates no direct signal.

This mission was carried out at the Direction de l'Innovation of ALTEN, within the Smart
Green Supply Chain Digital Twin research programme, between 13 April and 16 October 2026.
Its subject is the layer that sits before any planning decision: the urgency signal itself.
One question drove the work.

\begin{definitionBox}
\noindent\emph{Can the gap between what an operator declares and what the operational state
actually supports be measured well enough, inside a working digital twin, to be acted upon?}
\end{definitionBox}

Answering it took two steps, and the report is built on that division.

The first step was to build the tool. SupplyScore computes urgency from six indicator blocks,
elicits what the operator declares, propagates both across a supplier network, and runs the
whole cycle week by week while auditing every write. That step is engineering, and
Sections~\ref{sec:realisation_modele} and~\ref{sec:realisation_systeme} report it.

The second step was to find out whether the number the tool produces is worth anything, and
that is where the mission's contribution lies. A number a system computes is not yet a number
anyone should trust, so four tests were designed to try to break the tool rather than to show
it working. A pre-registered serious-game campaign replayed a documented industrial crisis
with independent declarants, and scored the signal against outcomes that were recorded rather
than asserted. A second supply chain was then built on deliberately different assumptions, to
find out whether the first result was a property of the tool or of the scenario it was born
in. A one-line baseline was written to ask whether the most expensive component of the system
earns its cost. And a parallel workstream went looking for an indicator no operator supplies
at all, estimated from satellite imagery.

All four returned an answer, and in each case that answer proved more useful than the
confirmation it might have given instead. What they established, and the boundary each one
drew around the tool, is what this report is for.

One result was not planned at all, and it is the one with the widest consequences for anyone
deploying such a system. Showing an operator the tool's own forecast, immediately before
asking them to declare, changes what they declare.
Section~\ref{sec:realisation_resultats} reports what it changes and in which direction, at two
different scales, and Section~\ref{sec:perspectives} says what an advisory layer owes the
person reading it.

The report is organised as the mission was run. Sections~\ref{sec:entreprise}
to~\ref{sec:organisation} set the company, the problem, the assignment and how the project was
run. Section~\ref{sec:etatdelart} reviews the existing methods, published and patented, and
identifies what none of them provides. Section~\ref{sec:realisation} reports the work and what
the four tests returned. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} assesses the results against the
objectives and closes with a personal assessment against the ESTIA competency framework.
