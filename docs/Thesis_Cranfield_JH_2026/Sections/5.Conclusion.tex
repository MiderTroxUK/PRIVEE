\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
%% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This research set out to determine whether the gap between declared and computed urgency can
be measured reliably enough, inside a working digital twin, to be used as a decision aid
rather than as a descriptive statistic. The answer has two parts that must not be run
together. As a device for deciding where to look, it works: the nodes the adequacy signal
flags in the six turns before the shortage becomes public are, without a single false alarm,
nodes that go on to miss a delivery commitment, and the worst-scoring of them is flagged eight
turns ahead while its own manager reports that all is well. As a device for attaching a
calibrated probability to a named week, it does not work yet, the Brier skill remaining
negative after a repair that halved it. The first is the decision a coordinator actually
takes; the second is a harder problem the campaign turned from an unknown into a measured
distance.

What bounds both statements is the evidence base rather than the design: two chains, seven
positives and thirty-three observable milestones cannot establish a rate, however clean the
pattern. The five contributions below are therefore claimed as a working instrument with a
measured behaviour and a method for interrogating it, not as validated performance figures.

Table~\ref{tab:contributions} states each of the five with the measurement behind it and the
bound on it. Two of them turned on a reversal rather than a number, and those are argued
after the table.

\paragraph{The repair changed the recommendation, not only the model.}
Making the events write a lead-time impact is the obvious reading of the C3 diagnosis, and it
does not work: a quantity routed into the lead time is scaled by the fraction of work
remaining, so it attenuates to nothing on exactly the milestones nearest their deadline. Lost
time has to be added to the completion date instead. The diagnosis was right and the
instruction it implied was wrong, which is only visible because the repair was re-measured on
the identical frozen forecasts rather than assumed to work. Two further defects surfaced with
it: the forecast treated declared progress as exact, which made the milestone probability
binary in $85\%$ of cases, and the campaign ran at $25.6\%$ indicator coverage with three of
six blocks inert, a finding about the data rather than the mathematics.

\paragraph{The same trap was met from the other side.}
A post-hoc calibration layer fitted against a different target improved calibration while
degrading ranking, and was deliberately not connected. Read with C5, the pair states the rule:
a calibration figure reported without its outcome definition carries little information, and a
calibration layer fitted without checking its target can degrade a system that works.

A sixth result is reported as an opening rather than a contribution. Individual loading docks
are not observable from directly overhead, because of canopy occlusion rather than
insufficient resolution, and the reframed problem of delimiting an equipped facade is both
learnable and sufficient for capacity estimation.

compared, which is a design fault in the scenario rather than a result.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}
%% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The work divides into what must happen before the framework can be evaluated fairly, and
what becomes worth doing afterwards. Two items dominate both lists and are stated first
because every other entry is bounded by them.

Table~\ref{tab:futurework} gives what each item takes and whether it must precede a fair
evaluation. Two carry an argument
a table cannot hold, and follow it.

\paragraph{Why F1 is the sharpest question and not merely the first.}
The second chain established that $[U_r - U_d]_+$ selects failing nodes when declarants
misjudge their own state, and selects nothing when they judge it correctly and choose not to
escalate. Both are concealment operationally, and only the first is visible to a
questionnaire, because a questionnaire records what an actor perceives while withholding is
something an actor does. That makes F1 a question about \emph{what} is being measured rather
than about how well, which is why no improvement to the model reaches it. The free-text route
carries one caveat: a measure taken on generated text has to be validated against human
declarants before it can carry weight.

\paragraph{Why more data does not settle F2 on its own.}
Two scenarios authored by the same hand cannot separate a property of supply chains from a
property of the author, and running a third by the same route would not either. A campaign an
order of magnitude larger in positives would let the node-level result be stated as a rate
with an interval, and would let the calibration layer be trained on the product's own target,
which is the single step now blocking a positive skill. Both gains are real and neither
touches the authorship problem, which only an instrumented live chain reaches.

One governance question belongs with F7 rather than after it: the detection library is
distributed under a copyleft licence, immaterial for internal use and determinative the moment
the capability is distributed or exposed over a network.

\subsection{Personal Conclusion}
\label{sec:personal}

\subsubsection{Technical Skills Developed}

The project drew on five distinct areas, and the difficulty of the subject lay mostly in
making them work together rather than in any one of them: multi-criteria decision analysis,
through AHP and Fuzzy BWM elicitation, consistency validation and PROMETHEE~II outranking;
probabilistic modelling, through noisy-OR aggregation, Monte Carlo simulation, conjugate
Bayesian revision in a sparse-data regime and, added during the campaign analysis,
probabilistic forecast verification through proper scoring rules and reliability
decomposition; graph algorithms, through propagation over a multi-rank directed acyclic graph,
topological ordering, shock simulation and network criticality; software engineering, through
the architecture of a multi-tenant Python system, hardened SQLite persistence, a Dash web
interface and a milestone-based quality process with blocking test coverage; and geospatial
data engineering, through public web services, dataset construction and annotation, and the
training of a segmentation model under a strict CPU budget.

\subsubsection{What I Learned}

The hardest problem was not mathematical. It was recognising, three separate times, that the
system was measuring itself. The dry run anchored declared urgency on real urgency by
construction; the agents in arm A read their briefings so carefully that their declaration
reproduced the computed state; and arm B closed the loop deliberately by showing the model's
own output to the declarant. Each time the symptom looked like a modelling problem and each
time the cause was circularity in the measurement. I would now design the independence of
the two signals first, before designing either of them.

The second lesson concerns what counts as a result. For a long stretch of the project I
treated a negative outcome as a failure to be fixed before reporting. The most useful
findings in this thesis are negative: the forecast layer is badly calibrated, the confident
tail is entirely false, one block is structurally blind, and showing that forecast to a
declarant is counterproductive. None of those would have been found by a project optimising
for a positive result, and each of them points at a specific repair, which a
marginal improvement would not have. The related discipline, which the annotation episode of
Section~\ref{sec:res_volume} taught bluntly, is that a confident self-report is not evidence,
whether it comes from an automated process or from oneself, and that the cheap verification
step should always come before the expensive one.

With hindsight I would change two things. I would have scored the forecast layer against
recorded outcomes far earlier, since doing so took a few hundred lines and revealed the time
block defect immediately, where months of implementation had not. And I would have written
the outcome definition into the pre-registration alongside the thresholds, having since
measured how much it moves the answer.

The skill that progressed most was not a technical one. It was the willingness to state a
result that does not flatter the work, in enough detail that someone else can act on it. The
subject of this thesis is, after all, the gap between what people declare and what is
actually true, and it would be a poor outcome to have measured that gap in a supply chain
while reproducing it in the reporting.

Professionally, the project confirmed a direction. I want to work where a model meets the
person who has to act on it, because that boundary is where the interesting failures live:
not in the mathematics, which can be checked, but in what a number does to the judgement of
the person reading it.
