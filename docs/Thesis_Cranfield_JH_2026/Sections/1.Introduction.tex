\subsection{Industrial and Technical Context}
\label{sec:intro_context}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Supply chain planning is a multi-dimensional assessment problem. An operator deciding which
of two late shipments to expedite weighs cost, deadline pressure, supplier reliability and,
increasingly, carbon footprint, while most monitoring tools still process one criterion at a
time~\autocite{pinedo2012scheduling,chakrabortty2020efficient}. Practitioners close the gap
with judgement, which is why the quality of that judgement is an engineering concern.

This thesis was produced during an R\&D placement in the \gls{din} of the ALTEN Group, whose
Smart Green Supply Chain programme~\autocite{benzakour2024history} pursues three aims: lean,
attacking cost, delay and over-stock; green, attacking the carbon footprint of each transport
leg to the \gls{glec} Framework v3.2~\autocite{smartfreight2023glec} and
ISO~14083:2023~\autocite{iso14083}; and predictive, anticipating disruption through
probabilistic modelling in the manner of a digital supply chain
twin~\autocite{ivanov2020digital}. This work sits inside the third, against the
Industry~4.0 premise that cyber-physical systems and digital twins can turn a reactive supply
chain into a self-organising one~\autocite{kagermann2013industry}.

\label{sec:intro_disco}
The programme's existing platform, DISCO, is a serious game and digital twin simulating a
complete supply chain for a fictitious satellite product across 22 component
suppliers~\autocite{baxas2025architecture}. Players act as supply chain buyers and decide
supplier selection, transport routes and factory allocation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{Images/1.Introduction/disco_map.png}
    \caption{The DISCO serious game network}
    \label{fig:disco_map}
\end{figure}

Its scoring engine applies Min-Max normalisation over the values of the current game
session, illustrated here on the carbon dimension:

\begin{equation}
    \text{ScoreCO}_2 = 1 + 9 \times \frac{\text{realCO}_2 - \text{co}_{2,\min}}
                                         {\text{co}_{2,\max} - \text{co}_{2,\min}},
    \label{eq:score_co2}
\end{equation}

with analogous formulae for cost and risk, on a scale from 1 for the best achievable
performance to 10 for the worst.

This work sits within a body of prior DIN research. \textcite{baxas2025architecture} defines
the DISCO software architecture including the \gls{tts} and \gls{ttr} formalisation;
\textcite{baulain2024internet} developed the Physical Internet routing module, with transit
times through the HERE Maps API, Vincenty distances, mandatory \gls{hgv} pause compliance and
the \gls{ipg}; \textcite{kabouche2025scoring} the supplier risk
scorer combining delay history, geographic vulnerability and financial fragility;
\textcite{cordova2024green} the per-route CO\textsubscript{2} quantification module on the GLEC
and VECTO methodology; and \textcite{mondelice2025tms} the Transport Management System
interface providing real-time transit-time extraction. The system delivered here, SupplyScore,
is a standalone Python application that runs alongside these modules rather than inside them.
Their coupling is a stated perspective of the programme, not a delivered capability, and this
thesis makes no claim to the contrary.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro_problem}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This thesis addresses the layer that comes \emph{before} any planning decision: the urgency
signal is systematically biased at the source, and must be measured before it can safely
inform anything downstream~\autocite{zhu2018mere,rusou2020psychology}. Where capacity is
shared the bias does not stay local. Over-triage takes capacity from whoever needed it more;
under-triage is worse, because a risk nobody has declared is a risk nobody is
watching~\autocite{li2020exploring,ivanov2020digital}.

Digital twins and real-time decision support are strong at operational monitoring, disruption
detection and resource
reallocation~\autocite{ding2023realtime,leung2022digital,hrusovsky2021realtime,karam2022realtime,zhu2025industry5},
and they do it by computing the physical state. To the best of the review in
Section~\ref{sec:Literature}, none models the gap between a declared urgency and a computed
one. Figure~\ref{fig:urgency_gap} shows that gap over the life of a task: where the declared
step sits above the computed curve the operator claims more urgency than the state justifies,
and where the curve sits above the step the state is worse than anyone has declared.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Images/2.Problem/urgency_gap_en.png}
    \caption{Declared and real urgency, and the two mismatch pathologies}
    \label{fig:urgency_gap}
\end{figure}

Formally, let $U_d \in [0,1]$ denote the declared urgency and $U_r(t) \in [0,1]$ the real
urgency computed from operational data in the digital twin. The mismatch between the two
signals is

\begin{equation}
    \Delta U(t) = U_d - U_r(t) \in [-1,\, 1].
    \label{eq:delta_u}
\end{equation}

A tolerance band $\varepsilon \in [0,1]$ is introduced so that small deviations are not
treated as operationally meaningful. Over-declaration then corresponds to
$\Delta U(t) > \varepsilon$, under-declaration to $\Delta U(t) < -\varepsilon$, and
approximate agreement to $|\Delta U(t)| \le \varepsilon$.

The two conditions need not cost the same. Weighting them asymmetrically, with
$\lambda_{\text{under}} \ge \lambda_{\text{over}}$, prices under-prioritisation above
over-prioritisation. Two bodies of work support doing so: operations research showing that
decision errors carry unequal downstream
consequences~\autocite{kim2020admission,diwa2020task}, and scheduling work that models
differentiated tardiness penalties instead of a uniform delay
cost~\autocite{baker1982dynamic,chakrabortty2022}. How much asymmetry is right is a
calibration question, answerable only in a specific setting.

\subsection{Aim, Objectives and Scope}
\label{sec:intro_aim}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The aim is to establish whether the divergence between declared and computed urgency is
measurable to a standard that would justify acting on it. That wording commits to a
falsifiable target rather than to building a system: a score that cannot beat a trivial
baseline against recorded outcomes has not met the standard, however well engineered the
system producing it. It also makes measurability the question rather than assuming it,
because an architecture that lets the same person supply both signals is measuring its own
input.

Three objectives follow. \textbf{O1}, define a multi-block, DAG-propagated real urgency
$U_r(t)$ and a cognitive adequacy score $A(t)$ measuring its gap with the planner's declared
urgency $U_d$. \textbf{O2}, implement network propagation and criticality-driven
prioritisation to identify structurally critical nodes. \textbf{O3}, validate the resulting
signal against recorded outcomes, and measure what happens to declaration behaviour when the
system's own forecast is placed in front of the declarant.

They are pursued through four questions: how a physically grounded $U_r$ can be computed from
heterogeneous KPI blocks and propagated across a multi-tier graph; how $A$ should be
formulated to penalise under-triage asymmetrically and whether it has predictive validity;
how pairwise elicitation and outranking identify structurally critical nodes; and how the
framework behaves when operationalised as a weekly-cadence serious game, including what
happens to declared urgency once the forecast is shown. Six hypotheses with numeric
thresholds were frozen before any data existed; Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses} states them with
the campaign they belong to, and Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis_status} is the only place their
verdicts appear.

Four boundaries bound what the results support. The declarants are language-model agents, not
human consultants, and the human campaign for which the protocol was frozen has not been run.
The evidence is two scenarios, an eight-node satellite chain and a fifteen-node airframer
chain, with seven positive outcomes between them. SupplyScore is standalone: it does not
integrate with DISCO, an ERP, or the routing and carbon modules above, and every KPI reaches
it through a weekly review or a scripted event. And the satellite observation work of
Appendix~\ref{app:opening} is reported as an opening, not a second contribution, with no
claim that the two systems have been connected.

\subsection{Thesis Structure}
\label{sec:intro_structure}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Section~\ref{sec:Literature} reviews the streams that bear on the problem and states the gap
each leaves. Section~\ref{sec:Methods} keeps three things apart that are easily confused: the
mathematical model, the choices made in building it into software, and the experiment designed
to test it. Section~\ref{sec:Results} reports what was measured, then diagnoses it.
Section~\ref{sec:Conclusion} states what the work contributes, what bounds it, and what should
follow. The appendices carry the apparatus, the full scoring tables and the two workstreams
reported as openings.
