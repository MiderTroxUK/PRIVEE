\subsection{Methodological Framework}
\label{sec:methods_framework}
%% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This research is structured as an agile test-and-learn programme rather than a linear
sequence, operating through repeated iterations of literature analysis, mathematical
specification, software implementation and scenario-based simulation. That structure maps
onto the IMRaD form of this thesis: the specification and implementation iterations are
reported here as the method, the simulation and campaign iterations in
Section~\ref{sec:Results} as the results.

The section keeps three things apart, because mixing them is what makes a framework of this
kind hard to read. Section~\ref{sec:math_framework} states the mathematical model on its own
terms, saying nothing about tooling. Section~\ref{sec:methods_impl} gives the application
choices, meaning how each object in that model is elicited, computed and exposed.
Section~\ref{sec:methods_campaign} gives the use case built to test it. The method's own
limitations, its reproducibility provisions and its ethical considerations close it.

\subsubsection{The Proposed Structure}
\label{sec:methods_axis}

The response designed here is a two-layer loop, and stating it before the mathematics makes
the equations that follow easier to place.

The first layer is a cognitive adequacy check. It captures the operator's declared urgency
$U_d$ through consistency-gated pairwise comparisons, computes a real urgency $U_r(t)$ from
live operational state, and scores their mismatch through an asymmetric penalty. The second
layer propagates and prioritises. Local urgency travels across the supply network, declared
urgency descending towards suppliers and real risk ascending towards the final customer, and
the resulting network state feeds an outranking and a shock-simulation criticality index that
together indicate which nodes warrant attention. Figure~\ref{fig:three_layer} shows how the
two layers sit relative to each other and where the declared and computed signals meet.

Two choices inside that design are worth naming immediately, since
Section~\ref{sec:lit_temporal} left both open. The aggregation over blocks is a noisy-OR
rather than a weighted average, because a complete capacity failure should not be offset by
acceptable cost and carbon readings, and non-compensatory behaviour is the property that
guarantees it. The penalty over the mismatch is asymmetric, pricing under-declaration above
over-declaration, on the clinical analogy of Section~\ref{sec:lit_analogy}, where
under-triage costs more than a queue. Both are design commitments open to challenge, and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} records what each one costs.

\subsection{Mathematical Model}
\label{sec:math_framework}
%% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This section states what real urgency and declared urgency are, how each propagates across
the network, and how their mismatch is scored. Appendix~\ref{app:worked} carries a single
node through every equation below, from its six blocks to the adequacy score its
coordinator's declaration produces, with the arithmetic shown at each step. It deliberately leaves out tooling,
elicitation mechanics and operational cadence, which are application decisions addressed in
Section~\ref{sec:methods_impl}.

\subsubsection{Local Real Urgency}
\label{sec:model_ur}

Real urgency is not read from a single deadline-proximity signal. It is aggregated at each
node from six independent KPI blocks, namely time, capacity, performance, risk, cost and
carbon, each producing a partial urgency $u_m \in [0,1]$, or no value at all when the
underlying KPI data is unavailable. The aggregation is a weighted probabilistic OR, so that
a single saturated block is sufficient to drive local urgency towards its maximum regardless
of how well the others score:

\begin{equation}
    U_{r,\text{local}} = 1 - \prod_{m} (1 - u_m)^{\omega_m},
    \label{eq:ur_local}
\end{equation}

where $\omega_m \ge 0$ is the aggregation weight of block $m$ and the product runs over
blocks with available data. Table~\ref{tab:ur_blocks} gives each block's schematic formula
and what it measures. The per-term weights $a_k$ and $c_k$ are renormalised over the
terms whose data is actually available.

\paragraph{Where the form comes from.}
Equation~\ref{eq:ur_local} is not an arbitrary choice among aggregators. It is what follows
from treating the six blocks as competing causes of the same event, and the derivation is
short enough to give in full, because everything the model can and cannot claim descends
from it.

\begin{assumption}[Independent competing causes]
\label{as:noisyor}
Delivery rupture at a node is an event $R$. Each block $m$ describes a mechanism $M_m$ that
can produce $R$ on its own, $u_m = P(M_m)$ is the probability that mechanism $m$ fires
during the period considered, and the mechanisms fire independently of one another.
\end{assumption}

Under Assumption~\ref{as:noisyor} the node escapes rupture only if no mechanism fires. The
mechanisms being independent, the probability of escaping all of them is the product of the
individual escape probabilities, so
\begin{equation}
    P(\text{no rupture}) = \prod_m \bigl(1 - u_m\bigr),
    \qquad
    U_{r,\text{local}} = P(R) = 1 - \prod_m \bigl(1 - u_m\bigr),
    \label{eq:noisyor_plain}
\end{equation}
which is Equation~\ref{eq:ur_local} with every $\omega_m = 1$. This is the noisy-OR of
Bayesian network practice~\autocite{pearl1988probabilistic}, and the name is literal: it is
the probabilistic form of the logical statement \emph{rupture occurs if mechanism~1 fires,
or mechanism~2 fires, or \ldots}

The exponent $\omega_m$ generalises that statement: $(1-u_m)^{\omega_m}$ is the escape
probability of $\omega_m$ independent repetitions of mechanism $m$. Setting $\omega_m = 0$
removes a block, which is how missing data is represented and is a removal rather than a
claim of health; a weight above one makes a block more able to drive the aggregate alone,
which is the model's only lever for saying that some mechanisms are more diagnostic than
others. The campaign runs at $\omega_m = 1$ throughout, so every result in
Section~\ref{sec:Results} concerns the plain noisy-OR of Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor_plain}.

\paragraph{Why not a weighted average.}
The alternative an engineer reaches for first is $U_{r,\text{local}} = \sum_m \omega_m u_m$
with weights summing to one, and it fails for a reason that is structural rather than
empirical. A weighted average is \emph{compensatory}: a block at $1.0$ can be pulled back
below any threshold by enough blocks near zero. Figure~\ref{fig:noisy_or_vs_average} holds five
blocks fixed and lets one rise, and the two aggregators separate as it does. At the right-hand
edge, where the deadline has passed and $u_{\text{time}} = 1$, the noisy-OR reads $1.0$ exactly,
because the factor $(1-1) = 0$ collapses the product, while the average is still at $0.40$ and
would rank the node below one that is mildly stressed everywhere.

    \label{fig:noisy_or_vs_average}
\end{figure} Physically the first answer is wrong: a
missed deadline is not compensated by a comfortable carbon budget. This is the
non-compensatory requirement that the composite-indicator literature raises as a caveat and
that Section~\ref{sec:lit_mcdm} traces through the outranking
methods~\autocite{greco2018,brans1986promethee}, and here it is met by the aggregator itself rather
than by a downstream correction.

The price is stated with the benefit. Independence between the six mechanisms is a strong
assumption and a false one in detail: a supplier under financial stress often has degraded
performance, so $u_{\text{cost}}$ and $u_{\text{perf}}$ share information, and counting both
double-counts the underlying cause. The noisy-OR then overstates urgency, and it does so in
the direction of caution rather than of complacency. Section~\ref{sec:res_armA} reports the
observed correlations between blocks, which is the check this assumption makes possible: two
blocks carrying the same information are visibly correlated on the campaign data, and the
assumption is falsifiable to that extent.

Three properties of Equation~\ref{eq:ur_local} matter for reading it, and they are also
where the model is most open to challenge.

\paragraph{What the endpoints mean.}
Each raw KPI carries physical units, whether days, euros, kilograms or percent, and passes
through a transfer function onto $[0,1]$. At $u_m = 0$ the factor $(1-0)^{\omega_m}$ equals
$1$ and leaves the product unchanged. At $u_m = 1$ the factor is $0$, so the product collapses
and $U_{r,\text{local}} = 1$ whatever the other blocks read. The upper endpoint therefore has
a precise operational meaning: the block has reached the state past which it cannot make the
node any more urgent, whether that state is a deadline already passed, a fully depleted buffer
or a near-certain failure. The word used for it here, saturation, is a label adopted for this
model rather than a concept imported from the literature.

\paragraph{How a missing block is handled.}
An unavailable KPI gives its block weight $\omega_m = 0$, so its factor is $(1-u_m)^0 = 1$ and
the aggregate is untouched. Absence is therefore not represented by $u_m = 0$, which would be
a positive claim that the block is healthy, and the two cases are distinguished in the data
model as well as in the mathematics. The remaining weights are renormalised, which keeps a
node with three live blocks comparable with a node with six.

\paragraph{Whether the blocks are exchangeable.}
The product is symmetric in its factors, so it treats the blocks as interchangeable up to
their weights. That is the strongest assumption in the model and it does not hold cleanly.
A value of $u_{\text{time}} = 0.3$ means a $30\%$ probability of missing the next deadline, a
quantity with a frequentist reading; $u_{\text{cost}} = 0.3$ means operating cost has drifted
three tenths of the way along a normalising scale, an ordinal statement dressed in a cardinal
number. No transfer function makes them equally informative about rupture. Any reading of the aggregate as a probability is therefore approximate, and it is used
for ranking and for comparison against $U_d$ rather than as a calibrated one. The
assumption is testable: two blocks carrying the same information are observably
correlated, which is the check reported in Section~\ref{sec:res_armA}.

Two lifecycle rules apply before aggregation. A node marked \texttt{DONE} has its effective
local $U_r$ forced to $0.0$, since a completed task carries no further physical risk, and a
node marked \texttt{ABANDONED} has it forced to $1.0$. These apply identically whether the
local urgency comes from the analytic blocks or from the Monte Carlo estimate below.

\paragraph{A stochastic variant of the time block.}
\label{sec:model_mc}

The analytic $u_{\text{time}}$ block assumes a Normal lead time at a single node. On a
multi-tier network that assumption breaks, since a node can only start when all its
predecessors have delivered, and the maximum of several skewed lead times has no convenient
closed form. The module \texttt{mc/lead\_time.py} therefore replaces the analytic estimate
with a stochastic one when enabled.

Each node draws $L_i$ from a configurable distribution, lognormal by default. Completion
times propagate in topological order, $S_i = \max_{j\in\text{Pred}(i)} C_j$ and $C_i = S_i + L_i$,
and the delay probability is the fraction of draws missing the deadline,
$u_{\text{time}} = P(C_i > d_i)$, over
$N = 10\,000$ draws by default, with a memory guardrail capping the simulation at
$N \times n \le 5\times 10^7$ across $n$ nodes.
The recurrence is implemented vectorised over draws rather than looped over them, so a
node's ten thousand completion times are one array operation.

Two properties of the estimator decide how it is used. It is a sample mean of Bernoulli
indicators, so it is unbiased for $P(C_i > d_i)$ and its standard error is at most
$1/(2\sqrt{N})$, which at $N = 10\,000$ is $5 \times 10^{-3}$ in the worst case at
$\hat{p} = 0.5$. That is an order of magnitude finer than the differences the urgency
signal is asked to resolve, so the simulation contributes negligible noise to
$U_{r,\text{local}}$, and increasing $N$ further buys nothing at the $\sqrt{N}$ rate. The
cost is $\Theta(N(n+e))$ time and $\Theta(Nn)$ space, the space being what the guardrail
bounds.

The default lognormal is parameterised by moments rather than by log-parameters, so that a
node's configured mean $m$ and standard deviation $s$ are the ones actually realised:
$\sigma^2_{\ln} = \ln(1 + s^2/m^2)$ and $\mu_{\ln} = \ln m - \sigma^2_{\ln}/2$. The normal
law is truncated by clipping at zero rather than by rejection, a documented choice: rejection
is exact but of unbounded cost, whereas the clip moves the mass $P(X<0)$ onto $\{0\}$, a bias
that is negligible while $m \gg s$ and never optimistic, since it cannot produce a negative
lead time.

\subsubsection{Why These Six Blocks}
\label{sec:model_blocks}

The six blocks are not an arbitrary KPI shortlist. The selection started from the question
that defines urgency in a supply chain: which mechanisms make the risk of a delivery
rupture rise? A rupture, meaning the inability to deliver at the agreed time, place,
quantity and quality, is the terminal event the urgency signal must
anticipate~\autocite{heckmann2015critical}. Working backwards from that event, and
consistently with indicator-based supply risk monitoring~\autocite{mokhtar2019supplier},
four direct causal mechanisms and two arbitration variables were identified.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Time erosion} ($u_{\text{time}}$): the remaining slack between deadline
    and realistic lead time. Time is the only non-compressible resource, so a task whose
    slack reaches zero can no longer be
    saved~\autocite{cohen2021assigning,bojarski2021,sun2020combating,sawik2013integrated}.
    \item \textbf{System saturation} ($u_{\text{cap}}$): queues, transport capacity,
    workshop load and bottlenecks. A saturated system introduces hidden waiting delays on
    top of the nominal lead
    time~\autocite{tomlin2006value,hosseini2019resilient,taghavi2024sustainable}.
    \item \textbf{Critical-resource performance} ($u_{\text{perf}}$): drift in the
    performance of the resource that produces the part. OEE is used as a composite proxy
    because precise performance data is rarely accessible on the supplier
    side~\autocite{mokhtar2019supplier,hlioui2017joint}.
    \item \textbf{Adverse-event exposure} ($u_{\text{risk}}$): the expectation of
    unavailability caused by a disruption. The default incident probability of $0.02$ is
    revised weekly from declared events through the Bayesian operator of
    Section~\ref{sec:weekly_cycle}, building on the DIN supplier risk
    scorer~\autocite{kabouche2025scoring} and on probabilistic ripple-effect
    models~\autocite{hosseini2020ripple,liu2021robust,hosseini2020bayesian}.
    \item \textbf{Financial flexibility} ($u_{\text{cost}}$): expediting surcharges, tariff
    rises and storage costs. Cost drift is not a rupture cause in itself but a stress
    indicator, and it bounds which corrective actions remain economically
    viable~\autocite{shen2019managing,tomlin2006value}.
    \item \textbf{Carbon overshoot} ($u_{\text{co2}}$): emissions above target. Like cost,
    it is an arbitration criterion rather than a rupture mechanism, becoming decisive only
    when a regulatory or ESG constraint binds and restricts the admissible
    responses~\autocite{hoen2014effect,smartfreight2023glec,iso14083}.
\end{itemize}

Each candidate indicator was screened against the four filter questions of
Table~\ref{tab:block_selection}. Only indicators passing all four were retained, which yields
four direct causes plus two arbitration variables and explains why the list has six entries
rather than four or eight.

The selection remains a conceptual construction until the underlying data is shown to exist
and each block is shown to contribute information to the aggregate.
Section~\ref{sec:res_discussion} returns to this, because the campaign measured one block
failing that test.

\subsubsection{Local Declared Urgency}
\label{sec:model_ud}

Symmetrically, declared urgency is not read as a single free-text estimate. It is
reconstructed from a structured elicitation so that it can be scored, propagated and
compared with $U_{r,\text{local}}$ on the same scale. At each node the operator compares
four criteria pairwise through the Analytic Hierarchy Process, which yields a
consistency-checked weight $w_j$ per criterion, then rates each criterion on a severity
scale $s_j \in \{1, \ldots, 9\}$. Local declared urgency is the weight-averaged severity
rescaled to $[0,1]$:

\begin{equation}
    U_{d,\text{local}} = \sum_{j=1}^{4} w_j\,\frac{s_j - 1}{8}, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^{4} w_j = 1.
    \label{eq:ud_local}
\end{equation}

The elicitation tool, its four criteria and the consistency gate are application choices
described in Section~\ref{sec:ahp_tool}. What matters here is that $U_{d,\text{local}}$ and
$U_{r,\text{local}}$ are both bounded, node-local quantities in $[0,1]$ before either is
propagated.

\subsubsection{Network Propagation}
\label{sec:model_prop}

Declared urgency propagates downstream, from the final customer at rank~0 towards
raw-material suppliers, while real urgency propagates upstream, from suppliers towards the
final customer. Both use a leaky product rule that keeps the signals bounded in $[0,1]$ and
monotonically increasing as more neighbours become urgent:

\begin{align}
    U_{d,i} &= 1 - \bigl(1 - U_{d,\text{local},i}\bigr) \prod_{k \in \text{Succ}(i)}
    \bigl(1 - \gamma_{ik} U_{d,k}\bigr), \label{eq:ud_prop} \\
    U_{r,i} &= 1 - \bigl(1 - U_{r,\text{local},i}\bigr) \prod_{j \in \text{Pred}(i)}
    \bigl(1 - \beta_{ji} U_{r,j}\bigr), \label{eq:ur_prop}
\end{align}

where $\gamma_{ik} \in [0,1]$ is the downstream attenuation coefficient on arc $(i,k)$ and
$\beta_{ji} \in [0,1]$ the upstream amplification coefficient on arc $(j,i)$.
Table~\ref{tab:propagation_components} defines each term, and
Figure~\ref{fig:urgency_propagation} shows the two signals travelling in opposite directions
across the same graph. Backup arcs are documentary and inert with respect to both
propagations, a modelling choice that the campaign scenario exploits deliberately.

Both recurrences are evaluated in a single pass each. The order of traversal is what
makes them computable at all: taken in topological order, every predecessor of a node is
resolved before the node itself, so neither pass ever needs a value it has not yet produced,
and no iteration to a fixed point is required.

\paragraph{What the recurrence guarantees.}
\label{prop:propagation}
Four properties follow directly and are used throughout. Taken in topological order the pass
terminates and returns the unique solution of Equations~\ref{eq:ud_prop}
and~\ref{eq:ur_prop}, since every predecessor is resolved before the node that reads it. The
result always lies in $[0,1]$ without clipping, because each factor
$1-\beta_{ji}U_{r,j}$ lies in $[0,1]$ and a finite product of such factors does too; the
implementation still clips, but only against floating-point drift. Propagated urgency is
non-decreasing in the local urgency of every ancestor, the partial derivative being a product
of non-negative terms. And the pass costs $\Theta(n+e)$ in time and $\Theta(n)$ in space,
since a topological order is $\Theta(n+e)$ and each arc is examined once.

Monotonicity is what licenses the criticality index of Section~\ref{sec:criticality}: forcing
a node's local urgency upward can never lower the urgency read at the final customer, so the
worst-case shock is well defined. The upper bound is what makes that index degenerate under
saturation.

\paragraph{Recomputing only what changed.}
A full pass is cheap but not free, and the weekly cadence writes one node at a time. The
engine therefore keeps two dirty sets and, on a write, restricts each pass to the cone that
can have changed: the descendant cone for real urgency and the ancestor cone for declared
urgency. By Proposition~\ref{prop:propagation}(iii) a node outside both cones has no path
along which the changed value could reach it, so reading its cached value is exact rather
than approximate. The restricted pass costs $\Theta(|C| + |E(C)|)$ on the cone $C$, which on
the campaign graphs is a small fraction of the whole.

\paragraph{When each quantity is computed.}
The weekly cadence governs when new data \emph{arrives}, not when the model runs, and the
two are easy to conflate. The sequence is as follows. A review submitted for one node writes
that node's KPI values and questionnaire answers. That write immediately triggers
recomputation of the node's own $u_m$ blocks and its two local quantities,
$U_{r,\text{local}}$ and $U_{d,\text{local}}$. The new local values then propagate, but only
along the directions that can be affected: a change in local real urgency is pushed through
the node's downstream cone towards the final customer, following
Equation~\ref{eq:ur_prop}, and a change in local declared urgency is pushed through its
upstream cone towards the suppliers, following Equation~\ref{eq:ud_prop}. Nodes outside those
two cones are not recomputed, because their values cannot have changed.

Two consequences matter for reading the results. Nothing is computed on a global weekly
timer, so the network is never in a state where all eight nodes were evaluated at the same
instant; a node reviewed on Monday and a node reviewed on Thursday hold values one review
apart, and the propagated figures reflect whatever the neighbours had most recently
submitted. And the campaign turn, not the calendar week, is the unit that makes the state
comparable across nodes, which is why every result in Section~\ref{sec:Results} is indexed by
turn.

\subsubsection{Network Criticality Index}
\label{sec:criticality}

The criticality service answers one question systematically: which node, if it failed
completely, would cause the most damage to final-customer delivery? For every active node
in turn, the service forces the node's local real urgency to $1.0$, re-runs the ascending
propagation, and measures two deltas, the rise of $U_r$ at the final-customer nodes and the
largest rise anywhere in the network. Every shock is run twice, once on the standard
pipeline and once on an $\varepsilon$-regularised twin that carries the same propagation in
log-survival, $\ell(p) = -\ln(1-p)$, with urgency clipped to $1-\varepsilon$ so that a
survival product is never exactly zero. Nodes are ranked by the rise at the final customer
first, then by the corresponding log-survival delta $\Delta \ell_{\text{final}}$, then by
name. 

One behaviour of this index matters for interpretation, and it is what the second sort key
exists for. A node already saturated at $U_r \ge 1.0$ yields a delta of zero and sinks to the
bottom of the ranking, since its risk is already realised in the baseline. In a network-wide
crisis, where many nodes saturate at once, $\Delta U_r$ collapses to zero throughout and the
ranking degenerates exactly where it would be most useful; nine of the nineteen campaign
turns are in that state, which is what the exclusion rule used by hypothesis H3 in
Section~\ref{sec:methods_campaign} was written for.

That degeneracy is not an accident of the data. It is forced by the model, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:saturation} derives it from the range and monotonicity established
above. Figure~\ref{fig:saturation} shows the two regimes side by side.

\paragraph{Saturation collapses the ranking, and the model forces it.}
\label{prop:saturation}
Suppose the baseline already reads $U_{r,f} = 1$ at the final customer. Raising any node's
local urgency cannot lower a downstream value and cannot push $U_{r,f}$ above its ceiling, so
the shocked value is again exactly $1$ and $\Delta U_{r,f} = 0$. The node was arbitrary:
every node scores zero and the ranking carries nothing. This is a consequence of the range
and monotonicity above rather than an accident of the campaign data.

The response is to measure the same shock in a coordinate that the ceiling at $1$ does not
compress. Writing $s_i = 1 - U_{r,i}$ for the survival at node $i$,
Equation~\ref{eq:ur_prop} is an identity in survival space, and taking logarithms turns its
product into a sum.

\paragraph{The twin is the same recurrence in another coordinate.}
\label{prop:logsurvival}
Writing $s_i = 1 - U_{r,i}$ and substituting into the propagation factor gives
$1 - \beta_{ji}U_{r,j} = (1-\beta_{ji}) + \beta_{ji}s_j$, so Equation~\ref{eq:ur_prop} is an
identity in survival space,
\begin{equation}
    s_i = \bigl(1 - U_{r,\text{local},i}\bigr)
          \prod_{j \in \mathrm{Pred}(i)} \bigl(1 - \beta_{ji} + \beta_{ji} s_j\bigr),
    \label{eq:survival}
\end{equation}
and taking $-\ln$ turns the product into a sum,
\begin{equation}
    \ell_i = -\ln\bigl(1 - U_{r,\text{local},i}\bigr)
             - \sum_{j \in \mathrm{Pred}(i)}
               \ln\bigl(1 - \beta_{ji} + \beta_{ji} e^{-\ell_j}\bigr).
    \label{eq:logsurvival}
\end{equation}
Each factor is a convex combination of $1$ and $s_j$. Clipping local urgency to
$1-\varepsilon$ therefore keeps every factor at or above $\varepsilon$, so no survival product
vanishes and $\ell_i$ is finite at every node. The twin changes nothing about the model and
everything about what survives the ceiling at $1$.

The twin therefore changes nothing about the model and everything about what survives the
clip. Two consequences follow, and the second is the reason the index has a second sort key
at all.

\paragraph{Off saturation the two keys agree; on it only one still separates.}
\label{prop:order}
The baseline is common to every shocked node, so ranking by either delta is ranking by the
corresponding shocked value at the customer. On $[0,1)$ the map $U \mapsto -\ln(1-U)$ is
strictly increasing, so the two rankings coincide exactly. Under saturation the first is
constant at zero while the second stays strictly increasing in the shocked local urgency,
because the clip acts at $1-\varepsilon$ on the local values and never on the accumulated
sum. The first claim is registered as a property-based invariant and tested on random DAGs
with random coefficients.

Part (i) is registered as a property-based invariant and tested as one, on random DAGs with
random coefficients. Section~\ref{sec:res_repair} reports what part (ii) recovers on the
campaign, where nine turns of nineteen are in the saturated regime.

Each shocked propagation is a fresh pass, so by Proposition~\ref{prop:propagation}(iv) the
index costs $\Theta\bigl(n_{\text{act}}(n+e)\bigr)$, doubled by the twin. No incremental
shortcut applies here: the shocks are independent what-if queries rather than a sequence of
updates, and each starts from the same unmodified baseline.

A second entry point answers the distributional question rather than the worst case. Instead
of forcing local urgency to $1.0$, it draws shock severities from the graduated flat-rate
severities of the calibrated event engine, minor, major and critical failures at $0.4$, $0.7$
and $1.0$ and financial alerts at $0.3$, $0.6$ and $0.9$, the two families weighted equally
and, within a family, by decreasing field frequency. Each draw is applied as a ratchet,
$\max(U_{r,\text{local}}, \text{severity})$, on the same principle as the event engine,
since a failure never improves a node's state. The batch is propagated in one vectorised
pass, in duplicate for both measures, and each node receives
$P(\Delta U_{r,\text{final}} > 0.2)$ together with the median and ninth decile of its
$\Delta \ell_{\text{final}}$ distribution. The computation is read-only, seeded and
deterministic.

\subsubsection{Asymmetric Cognitive Adequacy}
\label{sec:model_adequacy}

The mismatch between declared and real urgency is split into two named pathologies, false
urgency $F$ for over-declaration and hidden risk $H$ for under-declaration:

\begin{equation}
    F = \max(U_d - U_r,\, 0), \qquad H = \max(U_r - U_d,\, 0).
    \label{eq:fh}
\end{equation}

Naming $H$ a \emph{hidden risk} rather than a discrepancy commits the model to a reading of
where the gap comes from, and that reading is an assumption about the declarant rather than a
property of the subtraction.

\begin{assumption}[Sincerity of the declaration]
\label{as:sincerity}
The declared urgency $U_d$ recorded by the weekly questionnaire is a sincere report of what
its author perceives at the time of writing. A gap between $U_d$ and $U_r$ is therefore a
difference in perception, not a difference between what the author believes and what the
author is willing to write down.
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{as:sincerity} is what makes the two signals independent in source, which is
the precondition for their difference to mean anything at all: a declaration derived from the
computed state, whether by the software or by a declarant reading the dashboard before
answering, would make $H$ an artefact of the wiring rather than a measurement.
Section~\ref{sec:methods_controls} describes the controls that enforce the software half of
that independence, and Section~\ref{sec:res_dryrun} reports an early run in which it was
violated and the run discarded.

The human half cannot be enforced, only assumed. A coordinator who reads their supplier's
state correctly and still reports it low, because a recovery plan is not ready to announce,
produces exactly the same positive $H$ as one who has misread it. The two are
indistinguishable in the data this instrument collects, and they call for opposite
interventions: better information in the first case, and a different reporting incentive in
the second. Every behavioural reading in Section~\ref{sec:Results} presumes the first, and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} records that this is the boundary of what the design can
support. The free-text field beside each declaration is the only evidence that bears on the
question, and Section~\ref{sec:res_second_chain} reads three of them where the distinction
matters.

Drawing on Prospect Theory~\autocite{kahneman1979prospect}, the two are penalised
asymmetrically and with diminishing marginal sensitivity to large errors:

\begin{equation}
    \text{penalty} = \lambda_{\text{under}} H^{\alpha} + \lambda_{\text{over}} F^{\alpha},
    \label{eq:penalty}
\end{equation}

and the penalty is rescaled into an adequacy score $A \in [0, 100]$ through an exponential
mapping bounded by the penalty of a maximal one-sided error, with each term defined in
Table~\ref{tab:adequation_components}:

\begin{equation}
    A = 100 \times \frac{\exp(-\text{penalty}) - \exp(-\text{penalty}_{\max})}
    {1 - \exp(-\text{penalty}_{\max})}, \qquad
    \text{penalty}_{\max} = \max(\lambda_{\text{under}}, \lambda_{\text{over}}).
    \label{eq:At}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{What the score guarantees.}
\label{prop:adequacy}
At most one of $F$ and $H$ is non-zero, so with $\delta = |U_d - U_r|$ the penalty is
$\lambda\delta^{\alpha}$ for the $\lambda$ of the side in question. Four properties follow.
The score lies in $[0,100]$ and reaches $100$ only at $U_d = U_r$. It decreases strictly in
$\delta$ on each side. For a common $\delta$, under-declaration scores below over-declaration
exactly when $\lambda_{\text{under}} > \lambda_{\text{over}}$, which is the governance
constraint enforced at construction rather than left to configuration. And for $\alpha < 1$
the score is not differentiable at $\delta = 0$: both one-sided derivatives are infinite,
because $\alpha\delta^{\alpha-1} \to \infty$.

That last property is why the curvature is there and is not cosmetic. The score is most
sensitive where the gap is small, so a coordinator drifting slightly from the computed state
loses points fast while the difference between a large error and a very large one barely
registers. The signal is meant to catch a misreading while it is still cheap to act on, not
to grade catastrophes against each other.

Part (iv) is the reason the curvature is there and is not cosmetic. The score is at its most
sensitive exactly where the gap is small, so a coordinator drifting a little away from the
computed state loses points fast, while the difference between a large error and a very large
one barely registers. That ordering matches what the signal is for: it is meant to catch a
misreading while it is still small enough to act on, not to grade catastrophes against each
other. Part (iii) is the governance constraint stated as an inequality, and it is enforced at
construction time rather than left to configuration.

Figure~\ref{fig:adequacy_asymmetry} draws the resulting score. The governing constraint is
$\lambda_{\text{under}} > \lambda_{\text{over}}$, and the default ratio of $2.25$ reprises the
loss-aversion coefficient while $\alpha = 0.88$ reprises the curvature of the value function,
both measured by \textcite{kahneman1979prospect} and restated
later~\autocite{kahneman2011thinking}. These are literature-derived starting points, not
measured properties of supply chain coordinators, and both they and the aggregation weights
$\omega_m$ are configurable per project rather than hard-coded.

\subsection{System Implementation}
\label{sec:methods_impl}
%% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The model above is deliberately silent on tooling. This section describes how each object
it defines is elicited from an operator, computed inside the running system, exposed back to
a coordinator and kept safe. The delivered system, SupplyScore, comprises 73 Python modules
across 10 packages, covered by 1\,831 automated test functions in 106 test files, including
property-based tests.

\subsubsection{Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

Five decisions shape it, and Figure~\ref{fig:uml_architecture} shows how they relate. All
operations go through one orchestrating facade
(\texttt{services/}\allowbreak\texttt{orchestrator.py}); the web interface, the command-line
entry point and the test suite drive the system exclusively through it, and there is no second
write path. Data is split between one global registry database and one SQLite database per
node holding its private operational history, which prevents write contention between
operators and enforces the boundary that one supplier's raw history is not readable through
another's, and which is what makes each node of the campaign auditable after the fact. Every
business write crosses a single mutation service that whitelists the touched fields, validates
ranges, diffs against current state so a no-op leaves no trace, records author and source in an
immutable audit log, and synchronises the in-memory graph. The network itself lives in a
networkx-backed repository whose propagation is incremental, recomputing only the cones of
nodes whose local urgency changed. And each project carries its own clock, a system clock for
live use, a game clock advancing week by week and a fixed clock for deterministic tests, which
is what makes a compressed campaign possible without touching the model.

\subsubsection{Weekly Review Cycle and Calibrated Event Engine}
\label{sec:weekly_cycle}

The weekly review cycle keeps the twin's KPI state current without free-form manual data
entry: every write is structured, signed and auditable. At each new week every active node
is flagged as due for review, and the operator completes four sequential sections: the AHP
questionnaire, pre-filled with the previous week's values; the week's KPI values, shown
against the previous week; progress on active milestones, compared against theoretical
planning progress; and any supply chain events that occurred during the week. All four are
written as a single signed, atomic transaction, so no partial or anonymous review can
corrupt a node's history.

Rather than asking operators to recompute the KPI impact of a disruption by hand, the system
exposes a taxonomy of fourteen calibrated event types, each mapped to one or more KPI
updates through one of three calibration operators: a Beta-Bernoulli Bayesian update for
failure probabilities,

\begin{equation}
    p_{\text{post}} = \text{clip}\!\left(\frac{p\times n_0 + k}{n_0+n},\, 10^{-4},\,
    0.99\right),
    \label{eq:bayes_update}
\end{equation}

with a prior weight $n_0 = 26$, roughly six months of weekly observations; an exponential
moving average for continuous variables such as OEE,
$x_{\text{new}} = (1-\lambda)x_{\text{old}} + \lambda x_{\text{obs}}$ with
$\lambda \in [0.3, 0.5]$; and a ratchet update for severity,
$s_{\text{new}} = \max(s_{\text{old}}, s_{\text{event}})$.
Figure~\ref{fig:event_pipeline} follows one declared event through to the KPI fields it
revises, showing which of the three operators applies where.

\subsubsection{Explainability, Export and Operational Hardening}
\label{sec:explain_export}

When the twin flags a node, the coordinator's first question is why. The per-node
explanation page answers it with exact decompositions rather than approximations. Declared
urgency splits into one additive contribution per AHP criterion,
$\kappa_j = w_j (s_j-1)/8$, summing exactly to $U_{d,\text{local}}$. Local real urgency
splits the same way through the logarithm of Equation~\ref{eq:ur_local}: each block
contributes $\ell_m = -\omega_m\ln(1-u_m)$, the shares $c_m = \ell_m / \sum_k \ell_k$ sum to
one, and $U_{r,\text{local}} = 1-\exp(-\sum_m \ell_m)$ reconstructs the score exactly.
Figure~\ref{fig:explainability_waterfall} shows both decompositions side by side for a single
node. This is what allows a score to be traced back to the block that produced it without any
manual computation, and it is how the diagnosis of Section~\ref{sec:res_mechanism} was made.
Separately, one action compiles the registry and per-node history into a twelve-worksheet
Excel workbook so that analyses can run outside the tool on frozen data, and the persistence
layer enables write-ahead logging, checks integrity before every backup and copies each
database through the native \texttt{sqlite3.backup} API without locking the application.

\subsection{Experimental Design: The H\'ELIOS Campaign}
\label{sec:methods_campaign}
%% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Everything above establishes that the model is implemented, internally consistent and tested.
It does not establish whether the adequacy signal says anything true about an operator facing
a crisis. This section presents the experiment built for that purpose.

\subsubsection{Scenario and Network}
\label{sec:methods_scenario}

The first chain is HÉLIOS, an eight-node satellite supply chain carrying a semiconductor
shortage over nineteen weekly turns, with a foundry freeze as the scenario's one critical
event. The second is AIRB, an airframer's supply base of fifteen nodes and thirty arcs over
five ranks, with no exogenous shock at any point and three structurally central suppliers set
to degrade endogenously. Both were frozen and hashed before the first turn.
Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} gives the network, the calibrated event list and the turn-by-turn
injection schedule.

\subsubsection{Data Provenance}
\label{sec:methods_data}

The two urgency signals come from different sources, which is the point of the design.

 Real
urgency is fed from public time series of the actual crisis, namely INSEE production and
price indices, WSTS worldwide semiconductor billings and a semiconductor-shortage dataset,
frozen in a data pack whose SHA-256 hashes were recorded before the first turn. Declared
urgency comes from the participants through the weekly AHP questionnaire, with the
consistency gate active.

Every third-party dataset is passed through an authenticity check before use. A logistics
dataset initially considered for the freight node was rejected at this check for the
signatures of synthetic training data, namely pre-computed target columns, GPS coordinates
scattered outside the region the dataset claimed to cover, and uniformly flat
distributions. The semiconductor-shortage dataset passed, showing a secular price decline, a
genuine 2021 upturn and sectoral employment consistent with official series. The rejected
dataset is retained in the raw-data folder for audit but is never loaded by the analysis
pipeline, and the freight node's cost block is driven entirely by calibrated events instead.

Two provenance rules protect the real-urgency series. A single-pilot rule forbids double
counting: each KPI field of each node is driven either by a time series or by a calibrated
event, never by both, so when an event writes a field on a given turn the series skips that
turn. Second, one honest artefact of the real data is kept rather than smoothed away. The
INSEE business-failures series falls through 2020 and 2021 under public support schemes, so
a failure-rate indicator taken alone would lower external risk exactly while the sector
crisis was worsening. Keeping it makes the multi-block argument concrete: a single
macroeconomic indicator can tell the opposite of the ground truth.

\subsubsection{Pre-registered Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

The six hypotheses below were written with their numeric thresholds and frozen before the
campaign produced any data. They are reproduced unchanged: where the campaign later showed
a threshold to be badly chosen, or a hypothesis untestable on the scenario as scripted, that
is reported as a finding in Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis_status} rather than repaired by
rewriting the hypothesis.

\begin{description}
    \item[H1 (Cognitive latency):] Declared urgency follows computed real urgency with a
    delay of at least one weekly cycle on the majority of nodes. Test: the lagged
    correlation between $U_d$ and $U_r$ peaks at a lag of one week or more for at least
    five of the eight campaign nodes.
    \item[H2 (Early detection of hidden risk):] The hidden-risk signal
    $H = \max(U_r - U_d, 0)$ exceeds $0.3$ at the crisis-origin node several weeks before
    the rupture becomes publicly visible.
    \item[H3 (Structural criticality):] The systematic shock simulation ranks the true
    crisis origin first, by impact on the final customer, on at least 80\% of the
    informative, non-saturated weekly rounds.
    \item[H4 (Predictive validity):] At threshold $H \ge 0.5$ with a four-week horizon,
    the hidden-risk signal predicts recorded negative outcomes, meaning a missed
    milestone, a critical event or a node abandonment, with precision and recall both
    above $0.5$.
    \item[H5 (Urgency contagion):] On weeks when crisis news becomes public, operators of
    nodes that are not physically affected still raise their declared urgency,
    reproducing the mere-urgency effect~\autocite{zhu2018mere} at network scale.
    \item[H6 (Perception hysteresis):] Once the physical situation improves, declared
    urgency decays at less than half the rate of real urgency.
\end{description}

Section~\ref{sec:res_target_trap} measures its cost: the thresholds were pre-registered and the
definition of an adverse outcome was not, and that definition moves the measured result by more
than the thresholds do.

Distinct from these testable hypotheses, the framework rests on stated modelling assumptions,
each verifiable in the delivered code: the network is a directed acyclic graph, so a linear
topological sort is valid; reviews follow a strict ISO weekly cycle; declared urgency is damped
as it descends towards suppliers while real risk is amplified as it ascends, each scaled by a
per-arc coefficient; the six KPI blocks are treated as independent so a probabilistic
aggregation is admissible; task statuses drive explicit override rules; and the cognitive loss
is asymmetric, with under-declaration priced at roughly twice the cost of panic. These are
design commitments rather than empirical claims, and the limitations they induce are
catalogued in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{The Three Arms}
\label{sec:methods_arms}

The experiment compares three arms on the same frozen scenario, which is what allows the
effect of a treatment to be separated from the behaviour of the scenario.
Figure~\ref{fig:three_arms} gives the three loops.

Arm A is the control: eight isolated agents declare their urgency each turn seeing only their
own role card and turn briefing, and it is the source of the prospective forecasts scored in
Section~\ref{sec:res_armA_scored}. Arm B changes one thing, showing each agent the predictive
analysis of its own node from turn~4 onward, and adds one field to the declaration,
\texttt{influence\_prediction}, taking one of four values: no influence, confirms my
assessment, revised upward, or revised downward. That field is the direct measure of the
treatment effect. Arm C lets the agents act on the terrain through a catalogue of
interventions applied by the mutation service and recorded in an intervention journal, and was
executed in its agent version after the repair of Section~\ref{sec:res_repair}.

A rupture avoided thanks to an alert makes a good prediction look wrong, which is why the
comparison of arm C with arm A in Section~\ref{sec:res_repair} is made on final state, and why
the headline count of unfinished milestones there is not the figure to read.

Two properties of the design carry the results. The agents are language models, one per role,

instruction; a single model instance per role has no statistical standing as a substitute for

the influence field, which is what makes the two comparable.

\subsubsection{Scoring Protocol}
\label{sec:methods_scoring}

The forecast layer emits, for each node and each turn from turn 4 onward, a probability
$p_h$ that an adverse outcome occurs at horizons $h \in \{1,2,3,4\}$ weeks. Scoring those
forecasts requires an outcome definition, and this thesis uses the one the production
calibration service itself applies, so that the model is judged against the target it was
built for. A node-week is a positive observation when at least one of three conditions holds
within the window $[S+1, S+h]$: a milestone of the node whose deadline falls in the window
is abandoned or is still active with its deadline passed; the node is abandoned; or an event
of gravity critical or defect is recorded and not subsequently reverted.

Four quantities are computed, following standard forecast verification
practice~\autocite{brier1950verification,murphy1973vector,gneiting2007strictly}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Discrimination}, by the area under the ROC curve, computed by mean ranks
    so that tied forecasts are handled correctly, with a percentile bootstrap 95\% interval
    over 2\,000 resamples. AUC answers whether the forecast orders node-weeks correctly.
    \item \textbf{Calibration}, by the Brier score and its skill against the climatological
    forecast that always announces the base rate. A negative skill means the forecast is
    worse calibrated than announcing the base rate.
    \item \textbf{Reliability}, by a banded table comparing the mean announced probability
    with the observed frequency in each band. The bands are deliberately uneven, because a
    uniform ten-bin split hides the failure mode that matters here.
    \item \textbf{Operating point}, by the confusion matrix at the pre-registered threshold
    of $0.5$ used by hypothesis H4.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{How those quantities are to be read.}
Eight nodes over nineteen turns is a large number of node-weeks and a very small number of
events. Every table in Section~\ref{sec:Results} rests on a positive class of between one and
six observations, and at that size a point estimate on its own is close to meaningless. Three
rules are therefore fixed here, before any result is seen, and applied throughout.

Proportions carry a Wilson score interval rather than a normal
approximation~\autocite{wilson1927probable}. The reason is specific to this data rather than
stylistic: the counts of interest sit at the boundary, three of three and zero of three, where
the normal interval has zero width and is simply wrong. The Wilson interval stays inside
$[0,1]$ and keeps a defensible width at both endpoints.

Discrimination is tested rather than only estimated. The AUC equals the Mann-Whitney $U$
statistic divided by the product of the class sizes, so the null hypothesis
$\mathrm{AUC} = 0.5$ has an exact conditional test, and it is that test which decides whether
a reported AUC is distinguishable from chance. The percentile bootstrap interval is still
reported, because it conveys the spread, but it is not what any claim rests on: resampling a
positive class of size four produces an interval whose coverage is not the nominal one, and
Section~\ref{sec:res_power} shows the two disagreeing on this data at one horizon.

No claim is made from a comparison the design cannot support. Comparing two AUCs computed
against different label sets is not a paired comparison, so the three outcome definitions of
Section~\ref{sec:res_target_trap} are reported side by side and never differenced. Where two
scorers are applied to one label set, as in the baseline comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:res_trivial_baseline}, the comparison is paired and a permutation test on
the observed difference is the appropriate one.

The consequence is stated plainly rather than buried. With this many events the campaign can
establish the direction of an effect and the existence of a mechanism; it cannot establish a
rate, and no rate is claimed from it.

Separating discrimination from calibration is essential rather than
decorative~\autocite{murphy1973vector}: a forecast can order outcomes correctly while
announcing the wrong probabilities, and the two failures call for different remedies.
Because the outcome definition is itself a modelling choice, the same forecasts are
additionally scored against a deliberately naive target, any non-reverted event on the node
regardless of gravity and ignoring milestones. The difference between the two scorings is
reported in Section~\ref{sec:res_target_trap} as a result in its own right.

\subsection{Limitations and Simplifications of the Method}
\label{sec:methods_limits}
%% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Four simplifications are built into the method itself, as distinct from the limitations of
the delivered framework catalogued in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The six KPI blocks are treated as probabilistically independent so that the noisy-OR
aggregation is admissible. They are not independent in reality: a capacity loss raises cost,
and a performance drop lengthens lead time. The consequence is that correlated blocks
double-count evidence, which the composite-indicator literature identifies as the standard
failure mode of weighted aggregation~\autocite{greco2018,becker2017}. The explainability
decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:explain_export} is the mitigation: the coordinator sees
per-block contributions rather than only the aggregate, so a correlation is visible rather
than hidden.

The network is assumed acyclic, which permits a linear topological sort and makes
propagation deterministic and cheap. Real supply networks contain feedback, most obviously
in returns and rework loops.

The scenario is scripted, which is what makes the ground truth knowable and the forecasts
scorable, but it also means the events are the only source of adverse outcomes. The
consequence, quantified in Section~\ref{sec:res_armA_scored}, is that the positive class is
extremely small, and any conclusion about discrimination is correspondingly weak.

The declarants are language models. This buys perfect protocol compliance, exact
reproducibility and no scheduling cost, and it loses the property that matters most for
hypothesis H4, namely an imperfect human perception of a crisis. This is discussed
at length in Section~\ref{sec:res_discussion} rather than treated as a detail.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:methods_repro}
%% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Every numerical result in Section~\ref{sec:Results} is regenerated from raw artefacts by a
single script, \texttt{Utilities/figures/derive.py}, which writes a JSON file that both the
figures and the text of this thesis read. No number in this document is transcribed by hand
from an intermediate report.

\paragraph{Execution environment.}
Everything reported here was developed, run and measured on a single laptop: an AMD
Ryzen~7~PRO~8840HS, eight cores and sixteen threads, with $11.7$~GB of usable memory, running
Windows~11 and CPython~3.12.1 in a project-local virtual environment with a pinned lock file.
No GPU is used at any stage, including the segmentation work of
Section~\ref{sec:res_volume}, and no computation is distributed. Persistence is a single
file-based SQLite database per project, with no server process of any kind.

That is worth stating for two reasons beyond reproducibility. It bounds what the timings of
Section~\ref{sec:complexity} mean, since they are consumer-hardware figures rather than
server figures and would only improve on a deployment machine. And it settles the
deployability question the industrial supervisor asked at the outset: the whole system, model
and interface and database together, runs inside the resources of a standard engineering
workstation, so a pilot needs no infrastructure request.

The inputs to that script are the two campaign databases, one per arm, and the raw
per-agent declaration and forecast logs. The outcome labels are not reimplemented: the
script calls the production \texttt{CalibrationService} directly, so the thesis scores the
model against the same target definition the running system applies. Random procedures are
seeded, specifically the bootstrap interval on the AUC and the Monte Carlo lead-time
simulation.

Two provisions guard against silent drift. The script recomputes the arm A against arm B
forecast comparison on every run and reports whether the two arms carry identical
prediction-layer output, which they must since the replay is deterministic. It also reports
the outcome base rate at every horizon, so a change in the underlying database is visible
immediately rather than absorbed into a summary statistic.

One earlier analysis file in the campaign folder,
\texttt{analysis/rapport\_experience.md} with its companion JSON, holds a superseded run whose
participant-influence counts disagree with the raw logs. It is used nowhere in this thesis, and
the derivation script names it in an exclusion list so the choice is recorded in the output.

The satellite observation system of Section~\ref{sec:res_volume} is a separate codebase with
its own verification script. Its network calls are cached on disk, so a re-run does not
depend on the availability of the public services it draws from, and its calibrated
constants are stated in the source with the sample size behind each.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:methods_ethics}
%% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The research was conducted under the Cranfield University Research Ethics System.

 The study as
executed involves no human participants, since the declarants of both campaigns are
language-model agents and no personal data was collected, processed or stored at any point. It
was submitted as a Category~1, low-risk study, and the approval letter is filed with this
thesis; Appendix~\ref{app:ethics} records what the human version of the protocol would
require.

The protocol was originally designed for eight human consultants, and that version would
require participant consent for two features of incomplete disclosure, the cover story and the
two placebo sheets, together with the right to withdraw and a full debrief.
Appendix~\ref{app:ethics} records both and what each would require, so the obligation is not
lost if the human campaign is run.

Two further considerations apply to the work as it stands. The data behind the scenario is
public, namely INSEE and WSTS series and an open dataset, and the ALTEN material is covered by
the confidentiality statement at the front of this thesis. The satellite work draws only on
public French open-data services, retains no imagery of private individuals and operates on
industrial buildings; Appendix~\ref{app:ethics} states the constraints in full.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:methods_conclusion}
%% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The method combines a probabilistic multi-block urgency model, a structured elicitation of
declared urgency, a bidirectional propagation over the supply network and an asymmetric
scoring of the mismatch between the two, as a tested, auditable and persistent system.
Around it, a pre-registered three-arm experiment on a frozen scenario allows the forecast to
be scored with proper scoring rules, and the effect of showing it to the declarant to be
isolated from the behaviour of the scenario. What the method establishes is bounded by its
own design, and those bounds have been stated rather than left implicit.
