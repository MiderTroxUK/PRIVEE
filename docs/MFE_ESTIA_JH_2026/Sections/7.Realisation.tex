\subsection{The Model}
\label{sec:realisation_modele}

\subsubsection{Real urgency}

Real urgency is not read from a single deadline-proximity signal. It is aggregated at each
node from six indicator blocks selected from the literature of
Section~\ref{sec:etatdelart}: time, capacity, performance, risk, cost and carbon. Each
produces a partial urgency $u_m \in [0,1]$.

\paragraph{What the OR means, and why it is one.}
Read each block as an independent cause that could on its own put the node in trouble, and
read $u_m$ as the probability that it does. The node is then in trouble if the time block
fires \emph{or} the capacity block fires \emph{or} any of the four others does. The
probability of that disjunction is one minus the probability that none of them fires, which
is exactly the form the model uses, with an exponent $\omega_m$ setting how much each block is
allowed to contribute:

\begin{equation}
    U_{r,\text{local}} = 1 - \prod_{m} (1 - u_m)^{\omega_m}.
    \label{eq:ur}
\end{equation}

The \emph{OR} in the name is therefore the logical connective, not an arithmetic operation;
the literature calls this form a \emph{noisy-OR}. Choosing it over a weighted average is the
central modelling decision of the mission, because the two behave in opposite ways on the
situation the tool exists to catch. A weighted average is fully \emph{compensatory}: healthy
cost, carbon and performance readings average away a capacity that has failed outright, and
the node scores as moderately urgent. Equation~\ref{eq:ur} does not compensate, since a block
approaching its maximum drives the aggregate toward the maximum whatever the others say.

Figure~\ref{fig:ou} works both rules through on two nodes, and they rank them in opposite
orders.

Table~\ref{tab:blocs} gives the six. Four describe a mechanism that leads to rupture; two
constrain which responses remain available once it threatens. Each candidate was screened on
four questions: a plausible causal link to rupture, measurable or credibly estimable, open to
operator action, and adding information the retained blocks do not already carry. A block with
no data carries weight $\omega_m = 0$, kept distinct from $u_m = 0$: the first says nothing is
known, the second asserts the block is healthy.

\paragraph{The blocks are treated as exchangeable, and they are not.}
The product is symmetric, so it ranks the six blocks only by weight. Yet
$u_{\text{time}} = 0.3$ is a probability of missing a deadline while $u_{\text{cost}} = 0.3$
is an ordinal position wearing a cardinal number. The consequence was carried rather than
hidden: the aggregate is used for ranking and for comparison against the declared value,
never as a calibrated probability.

\subsubsection{Declared urgency and the adequacy score}

Declared urgency is not collected as a free-text estimate. At each node the operator compares
four criteria pairwise through AHP, which yields a consistency-checked weight per criterion,
then rates each on a severity scale. The four criteria were chosen so that each has a
computed counterpart: operational impact and downstream dependencies mirror the severity the
propagation amplifies, the time window mirrors the time block, and recoverability mirrors the
recovery term of the risk block. Without that correspondence the later comparison would be
between unrelated quantities.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{Images/4.Operations/questionnaire_annotated.png}
    \caption[The pairwise comparison questionnaire]{The pairwise comparison questionnaire, as
    the operator meets it.}
    \label{fig:questionnaire}
\end{figure}

A weight vector whose consistency ratio exceeds the threshold is refused, and the operator is
sent back to the most contradictory of their own comparisons (Figure~\ref{fig:questionnaire}).
Without that gate an incoherent judgment would propagate as though it were a considered
one.

Both signals then propagate across the network in opposite directions, declared urgency
descending from the customer toward suppliers and computed risk ascending the other way, since
demand pressure is what a customer transmits and physical risk what a supplier transmits.
Their mismatch splits into the two pathologies of Section~\ref{sec:pourquoi}, and the gap is
scored on $[0,100]$ through a penalty that prices hidden risk at roughly twice false urgency.
Figure~\ref{fig:gap} shows both signals over the life of a task.

\subsection{The First Campaign}
\label{sec:realisation_experience}

Building a system that computes a number establishes nothing about whether the number means
anything, so the experiment was designed before the system was finished. The campaign replays
the 2020 to 2022 semiconductor shortage over nineteen turns across an eight-node satellite
supply chain. Real urgency is fed from public time series of the actual crisis; declared
urgency comes from the participants through the weekly questionnaire, and is taken at face
value as a sincere report of what its author perceives. That hypothesis is what makes the two
signals independent in source, which is the precondition for the gap between them to mean
anything.

The foundry at the deepest tier is the expected bottleneck, and the campaign is built so the
tool has to find it without being told (Figure~\ref{fig:reseau}).

Four artefacts were frozen before any data existed: the scenario with its twelve calibrated
events, the data pack hashed per file, the role cards, and a register of six hypotheses each
carrying a numeric threshold. Freezing the thresholds in advance prevents a disappointing
result from being rescued by moving the bar afterwards.

The design compares two arms on the identical frozen scenario. In the control arm, eight
isolated declarants each see only their own role card and turn briefing and declare blind. In
the treatment arm, the same declarants are additionally shown the system's own predictive
analysis of their node before declaring, and record whether it influenced them. Because the
scenario is frozen, the forecasts are identical between arms, so any behavioural difference
belongs to the act of displaying them.

\subsection{Results on the First Chain}
\label{sec:realisation_resultats}

The results divide in two, reported in that order and both re-examined on a second chain in
Section~\ref{sec:chaine2}. The system does what it was built to do, and the predictive layer on
top of it was scored, its limiting component located, repaired and re-measured.

\subsubsection{The adequacy signal identifies the right nodes, and does it early}
\label{sec:ciblage}

A coordinator does not ask whether node $X$ will suffer an adverse event between weeks $t+1$
and $t+4$. They ask which supplier deserves attention this week, which is a question about
nodes rather than node-weeks, and the adequacy signal answers it. At each turn the eight nodes
were ranked by hidden risk, and the set flagged above $0.10$ was compared with the nodes that
would go on to miss a milestone at its originally committed deadline. Four of the eight did.

The flag does not come from a pessimistic declaration. It comes from an optimistic declaration
against a computed state that is not. A system aggregating only indicators would have ranked
that node by its indicators; a system collecting only declarations would have believed it. The
gap between the two carries the information.

The repair of Section~\ref{sec:reparation} sharpens the same flag without changing which nodes
it selects. The satellite prime scores $26.6$ out of $100$ at turn 3 against $46.0$ for the
next worst node; after repair the same turn gives $2.2$ against $44.6$. Identical ordering, an
order of magnitude more separation, which is what makes the flag readable on a dashboard.

One limit bounds all of it: eight nodes, four positive, one scenario. A precision of $1.00$
over six turns on three flagged nodes is an encouraging observation, not an established
rate.

\subsubsection{The forecast layer, and the definition it is scored against}

Turning that ranking into a probability attached to a named week is a further step, and
measuring it made the rest of this section possible. Scored against the outcome definition the
system itself applies, a missed milestone, an abandoned node or a critical event within four
weeks, the first version of the forecast layer scored below the reference on both axes that
matter, precisely enough to act on.

Discrimination is indistinguishable from chance: the area under the ROC curve ranges from
$0.37$ to $0.56$ across horizons and every confidence interval contains $0.5$. The positive
class holds between two and six observations, too few to conclude either way.

Calibration is clearer and does not depend on that sample. The Brier skill against a forecaster
announcing the base rate is between $-3.4$ and $-6.9$, several times worse than the trivial
baseline, and Figure~\ref{fig:fiabilite} localises it.

Where the model announces a low probability it is right: 74 forecasts averaging $0.070$ were
followed by an adverse outcome $8.1\%$ of the time. Where it announces a high probability it is
wrong every time: twenty-one forecasts announcing a mean of $0.948$ were followed by the
predicted outcome zero times (Figure~\ref{fig:fiabilite}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{Images/armA_reliability.pdf}
    \caption{Reliability of the four-week forecast}
    \label{fig:fiabilite}
\end{figure}

The two need different repairs, which is Murphy's distinction~\autocite{murphy1973vector}. A
forecast that orders outcomes correctly but announces the wrong numbers is fixed by a thin
post-hoc layer. One that does not order outcomes needs the model changed. Here the evidence for
the second is weak and rests on few positives; the evidence for the first is strong and does
not depend on that sample.

\subsubsection{Why: one block explains all of it}

The explainability layer localised the cause. Across all twenty-one confident forecasts, the
four-week probability and the milestone-miss probability never differ by more than $0.020$,
so the confident tail is the milestone channel and nothing else.

Reading the time block directly at the turn the scenario's critical event lands confirms the
diagnosis from the other side: the block reads numerically zero at six of the eight nodes in
the middle of a crisis. The cause is structural. The block computes milestone risk from a
lead-time distribution against a deadline, while the event engine writes to capacity, cost
and failure probability and never to any quantity on the time axis. The strongest form of the
evidence is a count: across the whole campaign, eight nodes and forty-one indicator snapshots
each, the accumulated-delay field the block would need was written a non-zero value exactly
zero times. No path connects a shock to the quantity the block reads, so the block cannot
respond to a disruption. It is blind by construction, and because it drives the entire
confident tail, so is the forecast. Figure~\ref{fig:chemin} draws the missing path and the one
that replaced it.

This is a defect in the model rather than a property of the scenario. Any deployment would
inherit it, and the repair is a change to the event mapping rather than to the mathematics.
It is the single most actionable finding of the mission.

\subsubsection{What happens when the forecast is shown to the operator}

For the first four turns the forecast is hidden and all eight declarants record no influence.
At the turn it first becomes visible, all eight record one, and the direction is entirely
one-sided: five revised their declared urgency downward and three had their existing
assessment confirmed. Not one revised upward. The first upward revision anywhere appears the
following turn, and the scenario's single critical event lands two turns after the moment
every declarant was either reassured or confirmed.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\linewidth]{Images/armB_influence.pdf}
    \caption{Influence of the displayed forecast, by turn}
    \label{fig:armB}
\end{figure}

Over the full campaign the balance is close to even, 15 upward against 16 downward
(Figure~\ref{fig:armB}), so the one-sided effect belongs to first contact rather than being a
permanent bias.

Two things sharpen it. The forecast displayed comes from the same layer whose confident
predictions never materialise and whose time block is blind to the shock about to arrive. And
it appears as a numeric probability with a confidence interval, a format the automation-bias
literature identifies as increasing
reliance~\autocite{goddard2012automation,bansal2021whole}. The system presented an unfounded
reassurance in its most persuasive available form, two turns before the event.

One qualification cuts against the strongest reading: the recorded field captures the effect
on the declared level, not on the action taken, and the free-text notes show declarants still
acting protectively. The effect is on the measurement, which is nonetheless the input the
whole adequacy layer consumes.

\subsection{The Repair, and What It Measures}
\label{sec:reparation}

The diagnosis named one component and specified a repair, carried out and re-measured on the
identical frozen campaign. Declarations and scenario are unchanged and the same one hundred
and twenty forecasts are recomputed, so any difference belongs to the model.

\subsubsection{Measurement corrected the recommended repair}

The obvious reading is that the calibrated events should write a lead-time impact, and that is
what the first version did. It does not work, for a reason that generalises. A completion date
estimated as a full production cycle starting now scales the lead time by the fraction of work
remaining, so anything routed into the lead time is scaled with it, and a shock evaporates
precisely on the milestones closest to their deadline. On the node carrying the scenario's
accident the four-week probability fell from $0.846$ to $0.000$ on a milestone that was in fact
missed. Lost production time is additive: a four-week freeze costs four weeks whether the order
is a tenth done or nine tenths done.

The completion model was rewritten accordingly, identically in the three independent estimators
the system carries for the same quantity, the analytic time block, the Monte Carlo rollout and
the PERT simulator:
\begin{equation}
    \text{completion} \;=\; t \;+\; (1 - \text{progress}) \times \text{lead time}
    \;+\; \text{lost time}.
    \label{eq:achevement}
\end{equation}
Five families of capacity-halting event now accumulate their duration into the node's
lost-time indicator: machine failure, accident, strike weighted by the share of the workforce
involved, material shortage weighted by criticality, and cyber incident. Loss of storage
capacity deliberately does not, since it removes space rather than production hours. On the
node carrying the scenario's accident, the first shock alone now writes $154.7$ engine hours.

\paragraph{A third arm, in closed loop.}
The repaired system was then run through a full nineteen-turn campaign in which the same eight
declarants also chose actions from a fixed catalogue, applied to the chain and journalled: 152

moved that node's four-week probability from $0.088$ to $0.214$ and the declarant replanned the
milestone. The chain from a scripted shock to a displayed number to an action taken now
closes.

\subsection{A Second Chain, and the Domain It Establishes}
\label{sec:chaine2}

A result measured on one scenario stays a property of that scenario until it is tested
somewhere else. The last phase of the mission therefore built a second supply chain, chosen so
that every design decision differs from the first, and ran the whole tool through it again.
What came back establishes where the measurement applies.

\subsubsection{What was changed, and why each change is a real test}

The second chain, AIRB, is an airframer's supply base: fifteen nodes over five tiers, from
titanium and composite suppliers through structural, hydraulic and avionics subcontractors up
to the final assembly line. Three properties separate it from the semiconductor campaign, and
each removes a support the first result might have been resting on, so that whatever survives
is a property of the tool.

answer. Twenty-two milestones are observable on the chain and five are missed, spread over
three suppliers.

The three suppliers that go on to miss declare the first, second and fourth highest urgencies
in the chain, from the opening turn, before anything has visibly gone wrong. A quantity
defined as the shortfall of declared against computed cannot open on a node whose declaration
is already near its ceiling: their hidden risk is exactly zero for all nineteen turns, while
the largest value the measure reaches anywhere on the campaign is $0.812$, on nodes that hold.

What makes this diagnostic rather than merely negative is the free text submitted alongside
those numbers. In the same forms, the three suppliers write that they intend to delay
informing their customer until they have a recovery plan to present with the bad news. They
minimise in action and maximise in measurement, and the two are not in conflict: a supplier
who has understood its own situation perfectly well, and is managing the disclosure of it,
declares high and acts late.

\begin{definitionBox}
\noindent\textbf{What the tool can see, and what it cannot.} The adequacy gap is a distance
between two numbers: what a node's state computes to, and what its manager declares. That
distance only opens on a declarant who \emph{gets their own state wrong}. It stays shut on a
declarant who reads their state correctly, declares a number that matches it, and manages
\emph{when} the customer hears the bad news. There is no distance left to measure.

\medskip

\noindent Two different problems have been separated here. \emph{Misperception} is a
measurement problem, and a questionnaire reaches it. \emph{Non-disclosure} is a behavioural
one, and no instrument that asks the supplier can reach it, because the supplier's answer is
precisely the thing being managed.
\end{definitionBox}

campaign recorded.

\subsubsection{Displaying the forecast, at five times the scale}
\label{sec:airb_affichage}

The second chain was then replayed with the forecast displayed, on the same indicator pack and
the same derived truth, so that display is the only difference between the two runs.

The first result is negative and simplifies the method for everything that follows. The
forecast scores identically in both runs: a Brier skill of $-1.601$ against $-1.592$ at one
week, an area under the ROC curve of $0.826$ in both, and an unchanged node ranking. Displaying
a forecast does not corrupt it, because the forecast is computed from indicators the
declaration channel never touches, so a treatment arm can be scored as a control arm. What the
display changes is the declaration, and at fifteen declarants the pattern is clearer than at
eight.

Table~\ref{tab:temoin} gives the comparison, with the better of each pair in italics. The
baseline ranks the misses better than five hundred trajectories at six horizons of eight, and
by $0.082$ of AUC at one week, which is the horizon a coordinator acts on. Since it reads the
outstanding fraction from the same progress variable the truth is derived from, it was re-run
in a form that reads nothing at all, taking outstanding work as the calendar fraction a
perfectly healthy milestone would show. Its AUC and PR-AUC are identical to three decimal
places at every horizon: the discrimination is entirely the ratio of weeks remaining to weeks
required, and no part of it comes from the indicators.

The same table says what the simulation layer does contribute, and it is not discrimination.
Its Brier skill is $-1.601$ at one week against the baseline's $-4.851$: the baseline produces
an ordering and has no means of turning it into a probability, which is precisely what five
hundred trajectories are for. It also holds its ranking at seven and eight weeks, at nearly
double the PR-AUC, where a calendar ratio collapses below $0.60$.

\begin{attentionBox}
\noindent\textbf{What this establishes, and what it does not.} On this chain and this
outcome, the forecast layer is a calibration wrapper around a lead-time ratio. That is a
defensible thing for it to be, and at long horizons it is the better instrument. It is not
what the interface presents, and it is not what the declarants of
Section~\ref{sec:airb_affichage} believed they were deferring to. 
\end{attentionBox}

Two limits bound the comparison. It holds for a chain carrying no exogenous event, where the
event term of the scored quantity is empty by construction; on the semiconductor chain that
term is populated and the argument does not transfer unchanged. And five positive observations
at one week make an AUC difference of $0.082$ an order of magnitude rather than an established
gap. The test was optional, and was run because a layer that cannot beat a two-number ratio
should not be shown to an operator as a probability.

\subsection{Problems Encountered and How They Were Resolved}
\label{sec:problemes}

Three difficulties changed a technical decision. The two others, documentary
access and the CPU-only budget, appear with their mitigation in the risk register
of Section~\ref{sec:risques}.

\paragraph{The first model could not represent the problem.}
The initial formulation expressed urgency as a single time-driven curve. It could not
represent a node on schedule but out of capacity, which is a common and dangerous state. The
resolution was to abandon it in April in favour of multi-block aggregation. The cost was
roughly three weeks; the alternative was building a validated implementation of an
inadequate model.

\paragraph{Keeping the two signals independent had to be designed, not assumed.}
A synthetic dry run written before the campaign derived the declared value from the computed
one, which would have made any gap an artefact of the harness. It was discarded. The
resolution was to state independence as a first-class design constraint: real urgency is fed
from public time series of the crisis, declared urgency comes from participants who never see
the computed value, and each result records which of the two could have reached the other.
Running the declarants as language-model agents is what made that iteration affordable. A full
nineteen-turn campaign replays in an afternoon, so a design flaw like this one was found and
re-tested the same day rather than the following quarter.

\paragraph{A test bench has to be able to contradict its author.}
Each of the three tests of Sections~\ref{sec:realisation_resultats} to~\ref{sec:temoin} was
designed while the outcome was still unknown, by someone with an obvious interest in one of the
two answers. The resolution was procedural and carries to any R\&D project: freeze the pass
mark before the measurement exists, define the outcome in the same document as the threshold,
and score any new instrument against the cheapest thing that could have replaced it.

\subsection{A Parallel Workstream: Capacity Observed Rather Than Declared}
\label{sec:realisation_volume}

The whole measurement rests on one hypothesis, and it is worth naming before the campaigns
that use it: the weekly review is taken as a sincere report, so the indicators it carries and
the urgency the same operator declares are two quantities rather than one. Putting at least
one of those indicators beyond the declarant's reach altogether makes the hypothesis matter
less.

A second system was built alongside the main work to do exactly that: it estimates the
physical capacity of a warehouse from public satellite imagery, an observation no operator
supplies and none can bias. Appendix~\ref{app:imagerie} reports it in full. Two
results carry. Counting loading docks from a nadir view is impossible, established by
measurement rather than by opinion: an empty dock door under a canopy registers $0.18\sigma$
of contrast against $5.3\sigma$ for an occupied bay, and 8~cm imagery leaves it equally
invisible. Restated as delimiting the equipped facade zone, the segmentation model reaches a
global bias of $-35\%$ on the derived dock count, which is directionally useful and not yet
trustworthy. The two systems have not been connected, and no claim is made that they have.
