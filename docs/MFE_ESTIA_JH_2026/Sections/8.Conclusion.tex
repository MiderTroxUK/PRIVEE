\paragraph{Quantitatively.}
The delivered system covers the specified scope, carries an auditable write path and was
validated at the intended scale. Two campaigns were run to completion across two supply
chains, 23 nodes and 33 observable milestones, producing 510 recorded declarations.

The measurements themselves are numbers rather than impressions. On the first chain the
adequacy signal flags three of four future failures with no false alarm over six consecutive
turns. The forecast layer's Brier skill improves from $-1.278$ to $-0.687$ at one week and
from $-0.553$ to $-0.216$ at four after repair, roughly halving the calibration deficit at
every horizon while remaining negative. On the second chain the adequacy signal reaches
precision $0.00$ against a base rate of $0.20$ while the forecast layer holds $0.56$, and a
one-line baseline outranks the simulation at six horizons of eight, by $0.082$ of AUC at one
week.

\paragraph{Qualitatively.}
The mission set out to determine whether the declared-versus-real gap can be measured well
enough to act on. It can, at the level a coordinator actually works at, which is deciding
where to look, and on a chain where the failing supplier is also the one misreading its own
state. The three tests that followed established where that claim holds, with a precision a
favourable score alone could never have given it.

What the programme can now say about SupplyScore is a domain rather than a score, and
Figure~\ref{fig:domaine} states it. The tool is worth deploying where all three left-hand
conditions hold, and not elsewhere.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion_limites}

Four limits bound what this report supports, and three of them are limits of sample rather
than of design. None calls the construction into question, and all three are answered by
running the tool on more chains rather than by changing it.

The declarants were agents rather than human consultants, so every statement about declaration
behaviour applies to that population. The positive class is small on both chains, four failures
of eight nodes on the first and three of fifteen on the second, which is why the precision of
$1.00$ in Section~\ref{sec:ciblage} is reported as an encouraging observation rather than a
rate, and why the AUC movements in Section~\ref{sec:reparation} are stated as uninterpretable.
And the carbon block remains the least exercised of the six for want of node-level data, the
gap noted in Section~\ref{sec:rse}.

The fourth is methodological and does not dissolve with more data. Every score depends on
which of the three outcome definitions of Section~\ref{sec:cible} it is read against, and no
number in this report should be quoted without one.

\subsection{Personal Assessment}
\label{sec:bilan}

\subsubsection{What I learned}

Three technical capabilities grew over these months. \emph{Supply chain modelling} came first,
and it is the substance of Section~\ref{sec:realisation_modele}: representing a multi-tier
network, choosing which indicators carry rupture risk, and deciding how they combine.
\emph{Software engineering in Python} came with the application, built to be handed on rather
than to run once. And \emph{applied artificial intelligence} came from two directions at once,
a probabilistic forecasting layer on one side and a computer vision segmentation model on the
other.

The methodological lesson is the one I expect to reuse the longest: the independence of two
signals has to be designed before either of them is. An early dry run derived the declared
value from the computed one and had to be discarded, and the forecast layer took a declared
progress figure as exact where it should have carried its uncertainty. Both times the symptom
looked like a modelling defect and the cause was in the wiring of the measurement. From that
came a habit that costs almost nothing: before trusting a new component, write the cheapest
thing that could replace it and score them against each other, which is what produced
Section~\ref{sec:temoin}.

With hindsight I would change two things. I would have scored the forecast layer against
recorded outcomes far earlier, since doing so took a few hundred lines and located the defect
immediately where months of implementation had not. And I would have written the outcome
definition into the pre-registration alongside the thresholds.

\subsubsection{What this mission clarified professionally}

I came into this internship expecting the interesting difficulty to be in the mathematics. The
mathematics behaved. The difficulty sits at the two joins: between a model and the world it
describes, and between a model's output and the person meant to act on it. A digital twin is
only as good as the understanding of the real operation behind it, and building that
understanding is where most of these six months went.

That is what I now understand the research and development engineer's job to be, as distinct
from the design engineer's or the consultant's: not only producing a system that works, but
producing the evidence that says where it works, in a form the next person can check. The
tests in Section~\ref{sec:realisation} are worth more to ALTEN than the application they were
run against, because the application is one programme's asset and the method transfers to any
of them.

The mission confirmed the direction I want to take and made it more precise. I want technical
work in simulation: turning the behaviour of the physical world into mathematical and
computational models, at the boundary where those models meet the people who act on them.
That is where the consequences are largest, and it is where an engineer can do most to shape
how tomorrow's operations are run.
